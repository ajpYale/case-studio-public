# Difficulty levels

## easy
A first-round warm-up. Interviewer-led. One clear question with one clear insight.
- 3-5 sections; 1-2 exhibits, each easy to read.
- 3-6 calculations, each one or two steps, round numbers, mental-math friendly.
- No distractor data, no pivot. The framework is the standard one for the case type.

## medium
A typical first-round MBB case.
- 4-6 sections including a brainstorm; 1-3 exhibits, one of which needs the candidate to pick
  out the right numbers.
- 5-8 calculations with a two- or three-step chain (e.g. segment revenue -> margin -> total profit).
- One clear insight that isn't the first thing the candidate would guess.
- Light ambiguity: one or two key facts only come out if the candidate asks a good clarifying question.

## hard
A final-round case or a tough first round.
- 5-7 sections; 2-4 exhibits, at least one containing distractor data or needing a unit or
  time-base conversion.
- 8-14 calculations with at least one chain of four or more steps (e.g. contribution margin ->
  breakeven volume -> share required -> feasibility vs capacity).
- A second-order effect the candidate must catch (cannibalization, capacity limit, discounting,
  mix shift, tax).
- A mid-case pivot or a new constraint introduced by the interviewer.
- Realistic, slightly messy inputs (e.g. 126 + 118 + 86 calls) that are still hand-calculable,
  and results that land on clean figures; candidate-led or mixed format.

## instant_offer
Exceptionally hard: the case where nailing it earns an offer on the spot. Harder than anything
in a normal casebook, but still fully solvable and internally consistent.
- 6-8 sections; 3-5 exhibits. At least one insight requires combining two exhibits; the data is
  deliberately messy (distractors, mixed units or time bases, a chart where the headline trend
  hides the real driver).
- 12+ calculations, including a chain of six or more steps and at least one of: NPV or
  perpetuity with a given discount rate, a breakeven under a capacity constraint, a mix or
  cannibalization effect, or a sensitivity / scenario comparison.
- A counter-intuitive result that overturns the obvious hypothesis (the analysis the candidate
  would naturally run first points the wrong way).
- A curveball pivot partway through (new information, a changed constraint, or a CEO question
  that reframes the problem).
- Interviewer pushback designed to test conviction: at least two "are you sure?" challenges with
  what a strong candidate should say.
- Ambiguous opening: the candidate must ask the right clarifying question to unlock a key fact.
- Synthesis under pressure: a 60-second CEO answer with a precise number, quantified risks, and
  a decision.
- The rubric should distinguish "strong" from "exceptional" (offer-on-the-spot) performance.
- Don't scaffold. Asks are open ("What's driving this?", "Should they build it?", "What would it
  take to change your answer?"), never step-by-step instructions ("first calculate X, then Y").
  The candidate must decide which analyses to run; the hard part is choosing them and catching
  the trap, not following directions. Hints can be more directive.
- Every section does a different job. One synthesis at the end; no two sections ask the same thing.
- Keep it hand-calculable: the difficulty comes from judgement and the trap, not from
  five-significant-figure arithmetic.
